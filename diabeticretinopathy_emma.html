What is changing institutionally?

Graders & clinical leadRoutine grading shifts toward exception work and audit. Retraining protects expertise; workload and workforce cuts erode it.
Managers & commissionersStaff savings can conflict with resilience. Integration and shared governance accelerate useful adoption and reduce incident disruption.
Vendor & referral serviceOutages and drift create fallback work. Faster grading may simply transfer the bottleneck to ophthalmology.

Model, assumptions and limits

This is a hypothetical automated image-grading intervention. Invitations, image capture, attendance and treatment remain outside the model. The unit is one screening episode, not one eye or image. All defaults and institutional equations are synthetic teaching assumptions, not estimates from NHS services or a particular AI product.

  1. Each future draws demand, disease prevalence, performance, staffing productivity and initial institutional states. The selected strategy and manual counterfactual share the same environmental random draws.
  2. Monthly uptake approaches the target at a rate influenced by interoperability, governance and trust. A random outage forces affected activity back to humans. A persistent performance-drift shock is mitigated by governance.
  3. Ungradable episodes bypass AI for human resolution. On AI-eligible episodes, positive flags and sampled negatives enter the human queue; unaudited negatives are released. AI positives receive human confirmation. Audited false negatives may be recovered by human grading.
  4. Monthly human capacity = available FTE × productivity × expertise × overload penalty. Complexity increases the work per review as automation concentrates exceptions. Reviews are proportional across a first-in, first-out queue. No automatic diagnosis is issued for ungradable episodes.
  5. Human sensitivity and specificity depend on retained expertise. Misses count disease in unaudited AI negatives plus false negatives in completed human reviews. Confirmed referrals enter a separate capacity-limited queue.
  6. Expertise erodes with loss of routine exposure and improves with retraining. Trust changes with engagement, overload, cuts and audited AI misses. Audit findings can trigger a three-month automated-grading pause; shared governance shortens disruptive responses.
  7. Cost includes FTE at £55,000/year, £100,000/year fixed overhead, AI licence per processed episode, £80,000 implementation and retraining expenditure of £15,000/FTE/year × retraining intensity. It excludes imaging, treatment, patient harm, severance and downstream service costs. The cost chart covers the grading centre only.

Uncertainty intensity controls parameter variation and monthly demand/performance variability. Monthly arrivals are a normal approximation to a Poisson process; cohorts use expected disease/decision fractions, rather than individual Bernoulli draws. Uniform random shocks model outages and one drift event. These choices reduce computation but do not represent rare catastrophic risk or correlated human/AI errors. Human and AI errors are conditionally independent in this simplified model. Queued ungradable images are assumed resolvable through human workflow; repeat photography and slit-lamp pathways are not explicitly represented.

Workforce cuts are implemented gradually in proportion to realised AI uptake, with a 15% original-FTE floor. Retraining removes up to 12% of human grading time. The fixed manual counterfactual uses the same initial staffing and demand, no AI, no cuts, and 20% retraining intensity. Success is a conjunction of the four editable targets and cost savings; changing thresholds changes its probability. The chart bands show outcome dispersion, not confidence intervals on the mean.

Institutional feedback coefficients are intentionally contestable. Use the app to formulate questions, compare strategies and identify local data needed for calibration; it does not predict blindness, clinical benefit or regulatory compliance.
